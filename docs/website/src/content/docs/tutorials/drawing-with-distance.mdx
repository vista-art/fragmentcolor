A triangle on the GPU is three corners and a rule for filling the space between them. That covers anything with corners. A circle has none, and neither does the soft bridge where a circle melts into a star.

This tutorial draws shapes the other way around. Every pixel runs the same small function and answers one question for itself: how far am I from the shape? The sign of the answer says inside or outside. Its size says how far. With that one number we'll draw a circle, a box and a star, merge them, paint them with contour lines, and set them moving with time and the pointer. The last step arrives at the playground's Shapes demo, one line at a time.

If you haven't yet, read [Hello, 15000 triangles!](/tutorials/01-hello-triangle/) first. This page assumes you know what `Shader::new`, `set` and `render` do.

## Every pixel gets a coordinate

A fragment shader runs once per pixel it covers, so the first job is a triangle that covers all of them. One oversized triangle does it. Its corners sit at `(-1, -1)`, `(3, -1)` and `(-1, 3)` in the `[-1, 1]` space from the hello triangle tutorial, which puts the whole canvas inside it. The GPU clips the parts that hang off the edges and runs the fragment stage for every pixel that's left.

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("01-coordinates", "triangle").rust} />

This vertex stage never changes again. Everything from here on happens in the fragment stage.

The fragment stage learns where it is from `@builtin(position)`. Its `xy` holds the pixel's position in pixels, counted from the top left corner, with y growing downward. The GPU samples each pixel at its center, so on an 800 by 600 canvas the top left pixel reads `(0.5, 0.5)` and the bottom right one reads `(799.5, 599.5)`.

Pixels make an awkward unit for drawing. A circle 100 pixels wide fills a phone screen and gets lost on a 4K monitor. We want coordinates that look the same on any canvas, and `center` builds them in three moves:

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("01-coordinates", "center").rust} />

1. Subtracting half of `resolution` moves the origin to the middle of the canvas.
2. Dividing by the shorter side makes that side one unit long, from `-0.5` to `0.5`. Both axes divide by the same number, which keeps a unit along x as long as a unit along y. The longer side gets whatever range is left: `-0.67` to `0.67` across an 800 by 600 canvas.
3. Negating y makes up positive, the way a math textbook draws its axes.

The result is `p`. Here it is painted as a color, with `p.x` driving red and `p.y` driving green. Inside the unit square the colors run at full strength; past its edges they dim to a third.

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("01-coordinates", "paint").rust} />

<Demo file="drawing-with-distance/01-coordinates/example.rs" />

Make the browser window wider or narrower and the bright square keeps its shape. A wide canvas grows dim margins at the sides, and a tall one grows them above and below. Had we divided x by the width and y by the height, the square would stretch with the canvas, the same way the triangle did before the hello triangle tutorial fixed its aspect ratio.

The host code is short. `setup` builds the shader once:

and `frame` runs on every redraw, with `size` holding the canvas size in pixels as `[width, height]`:

<Snippet file="drawing-with-distance/01-coordinates/example.rs" region="frame" />

Every program in this tutorial keeps that shape, and the playground's programs share it: a `setup` that runs once and a `frame` that runs on every redraw. From step to step, the shader changes and the list of uniforms grows. The rest stays put.

Before moving on, look again at the test in `fs_main`. `max(abs(p.x), abs(p.y))` is `0.5` everywhere on the edge of the unit square, smaller inside it and larger outside. It measures how far `p` is from the middle, in square steps, and the comparison with `0.5` decides inside or outside. That one line holds the whole idea of this tutorial.

## A circle is a distance

`length(p)` is the distance from `p` to the origin. Subtract a radius and you have a circle:

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("02-circle", "paint").rust} />

Points on the circle get `0.0`. Points inside get negative numbers, down to `-0.3` at the center. Points outside get positive numbers that keep growing toward the corners of the canvas. The size of `d` is the distance to the nearest point of the circle, and its sign tells you which side you're on. A function that gives a signed distance for every point of the plane is a **signed distance field**, or SDF.

To see the field, `show` paints the sign and adds a stripe every `0.05` units of distance:

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("02-circle", "show").rust} />

`select` picks blue where `d` is negative. `fract(d * 20.0)` climbs from `0.0` to `1.0` once every `0.05` units, and `step(0.5, ...)` turns each climb into a dark half and a light half.

<Demo file="drawing-with-distance/02-circle/example.rs" />

The circle's edge is the line where blue meets orange, at `d = 0`. Count the stripes from that edge to the center and you'll find six, one for every `0.05` of the radius. Outside, the rings keep their spacing all the way to the edges of the canvas. Wherever you point, counting rings back to the edge reads off the distance.

To move the circle, move the point instead: `length(p - c) - r` is a circle of radius `r` centered on `c`. Every shape on this page gets placed that way, by subtracting its position from `p` before measuring.

The shader library ships this function as [`sdf2d/circle`](/shaders/sdf2d/#circle):

```wgsl
fn circle(p: vec2<f32>, r: f32) -> f32 {
  return length(p) - r;
}
```

From here on we import it by slug, the way the hello triangle tutorial imported `noise/simplex2`.

## Corners: a box and a star

A box makes the question harder, because the nearest point might lie on an edge or on a corner. Symmetry does most of the work. A box centered on the origin looks the same in all four quarters of the plane, so `abs(p)` folds every point into the top right quarter, and the function only has to handle that one.

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("03-box-and-star", "box").rust} />

`q` says how far the point sits past the right edge (`q.x`) and past the top edge (`q.y`). A negative value means it hasn't reached that edge yet.

- Outside the box, at least one of them is positive. `max(q, vec2<f32>(0.0))` zeroes the negative one, and the `length` of what's left is the distance to the nearest edge, or to the corner when both are positive.
- Inside, both are negative. The nearest edge is the one with the larger value, `max(q.x, q.y)`, and wrapping it in `min(..., 0.0)` silences that term outside the box.

At any point one of the two terms is zero, and the sum is the other one.

A star needs more folding: around the center into a single point of the star, then across that point's middle. Inigo Quilez worked it out, along with dozens of other 2D shapes, and the shader library ships his version as [`sdf2d/star`](/shaders/sdf2d/#star). It takes `star(p, r, n, m)`. `r` is the distance from the center to each tip, and `n` counts the points as a `u32`, which is why the call below writes `5u`. `m` runs from `2.0` to `n`: at `2.0` the star is a plain pentagon, and larger values cut deeper valleys between the points.

To draw both shapes at once, keep the smaller of the two distances. The next section shows why `min` does the job.

The stripes are worth a long look. Next to each shape they copy its outline, pointed around the star and square around the box. Farther out the corners soften, and near the edges of the canvas the rings are almost circles: seen from far enough away, any shape shrinks to a point. Inside the box, the stripes nest as smaller rectangles that meet along the diagonals, where two edges are equally close. Between the two shapes, the rings from each side meet along a seam. That seam is where the star becomes the closer of the two.

## Combining shapes

`min` works because of what the sign means. A point lies inside the union of two shapes when it lies inside either one, and the smaller of two distances is negative whenever either distance is. Outside both, the smaller distance belongs to the nearer shape, and that's the distance to the union. Swap in `max` and the result goes negative only where both distances are: the intersection. Flip one sign first and `max(a, -b)` keeps the parts of `a` that lie outside `b`, which subtracts `b` from `a`.

The shader library names these three `op_union`, `op_intersect` and `op_subtract`. Each one is the single line you just read.

A union leaves a sharp crease where two outlines cross. `op_smooth_union(a, b, k)` fills it with a curve:

```wgsl
fn op_smooth_union(a: f32, b: f32, k: f32) -> f32 {
  let h = clamp(0.5 + 0.5 * (b - a) / k, 0.0, 1.0);
  return mix(b, a, h) - k * h * (1.0 - h);
}
```

`h` compares the two distances on a scale from `0.0` to `1.0`. When one shape is closer by more than `k`, `h` is exactly `0.0` or `1.0` and the function returns the plain minimum. When the two are within `k` of each other, `mix` blends them and `k * h * (1.0 - h)` takes away a little more, most of all where the distances are equal. A smaller distance pushes the surface outward, so the crease fills in with a fillet about `k` units wide.

`min`, `max` and subtraction keep the zero line and the sign exact. The size of the number can come out smaller than the true distance in places, inside a union for instance. For drawing outlines that never matters. It starts to matter once an effect needs the exact distance, so it's worth remembering.

## Coloring the field

The stripes in `show` are a debugging view. Inigo Quilez colors his distance fields with a few more lines, and the result reads at a glance. To try it on something worth coloring, the next scene merges the star and a box with a small circle between them. The circle sits close enough to both that the smooth unions bridge all three:

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("05-color", "scene").rust} />

The coloring builds up in layers, each line adjusting the last:

<Code lang="wgsl" code={region("05-color", "color").rust} />

1. `select` picks a tint by sign, as `show` did.
2. `exp(-8.0 * abs(d))` is `1.0` on the edge and fades to almost nothing a few tenths of a unit away. Multiplying the color by `1.0 - 0.75 * exp(-8.0 * abs(d))` darkens a band on both sides of the outline.
3. `cos(6.2831853 * 40.0 * d)` repeats every `1 / 40` of a unit, as `fract` did in `show`, but with soft edges. Scaled to swing between `0.7` and `1.0`, it draws contour lines. Each one joins points at the same distance from the shapes, like the height lines on a hiking map.
4. `fwidth(d)` measures how much `d` changes from this pixel to its neighbors, which is the width of one pixel expressed as a distance. `smoothstep(pixel_size, 2.0 * pixel_size, abs(d))` is `0.0` within one pixel of the edge and `1.0` past two, and mixing toward white by one minus that draws a line one or two pixels wide.

<Demo file="drawing-with-distance/05-color/example.rs" />

That last line keeps the outline crisp on a phone, on a 4K monitor and at any zoom, without the shader ever knowing the canvas size. Every shader that draws a thin line from a distance can borrow it.

## The Shapes demo

The playground's Shapes demo is this program with a hexagon from `sdf2d/hexagon` in place of the box. It reaches `p` from the vertex stage's `uv` output instead of `@builtin(position)`, which lands on the same coordinates. Its sliders come from the same `// min..max` comments: drag `blend` toward `0.4` and the shapes fuse, or drop `bands` to `5` and the contour lines spread wide apart. Every tab is editable. The WGSL rebuilds as you type, and **+ Add** pulls more slugs from the shader library.

<Playground demos={["shapes"]} />

The star and box distances, the smooth union and the contour coloring all come from Inigo Quilez. His page on [2D distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions2d/) lists dozens more shapes, from arcs to Bézier curves, each with a live example, and most of them carry over to WGSL line for line. Many already live in the shader library under [`sdf2d/`](/shaders/sdf2d/).

You can now turn any pixel into a coordinate that ignores the canvas shape, compute a value for it, and paint that value with care. Those three moves come back in every tutorial that follows, whatever question each pixel ends up asking.
