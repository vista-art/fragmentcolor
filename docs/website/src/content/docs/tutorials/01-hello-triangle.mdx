There's a rite of passage in graphics programming:  **Drawing your first triangle.**

**This typically takes thousands of lines of code.** Pick any tutorial for OpenGL, Vulkan, or raw WebGPU, and you'll spend an afternoon setting pixel formats, allocating command pools, and wiring descriptor sets & binding groups before any colored pixel reaches the screen.

**We're going to do it in 5 lines of <Lang/>**.

</Lock>

## Step 1: Hello, triangle!

To draw anything, anywhere, we need two things:

- a **surface** to draw on,
- and **something that draws**.

In FragmentColor those are a `Target` and a `Renderer`. Let's create them:

<MultiCode
  rust={`// canvas is an Arc<Window> from winit
let renderer = Renderer::new();
let target = renderer.create_target(canvas).await?;`}
  js={`// canvas is a <canvas> element
const renderer = new Renderer();
const target = await renderer.createTarget(canvas);`}
  py={`# canvas is a RenderCanvas from rendercanvas
renderer = Renderer()
target = renderer.create_target(canvas)`}
  swift={`// canvas is a CAMetalLayer, such as an MTKView's layer
let renderer = Renderer()
let target = try renderer.createTarget(canvas)`}
  kotlin={`// canvas is an android.view.Surface, such as the one a SurfaceView holds
val renderer = Renderer()
val target = renderer.createTarget(canvas)`}
/>

The `canvas` is whatever your platform draws into: a `<canvas>` element on the web, a `winit` window in Rust, a `RenderCanvas` in Python, a `CAMetalLayer` on iOS and macOS, and a `Surface` on Android. The `Renderer` wraps it in a `Target`.

Now, what do we draw? The answer is a **`Shader`**: a small program that runs on the GPU, the graphics chip in your computer or phone. A shader that draws has two stages. The **vertex** stage runs once per corner of a shape and decides where that corner lands on the screen. The **fragment** stage runs once per pixel inside the shape and decides its color. FragmentColor takes its name from that second stage.

We write shaders in WGSL, the shading language of WebGPU. Here's ours:

Keep that source in a string called <code><Lang rust="TRIANGLE" js="TRIANGLE" py="TRIANGLE" swift="triangle" kotlin="TRIANGLE" /></code>. Three more lines turn it into a picture:

**Congratulations! You just drew your first triangle!** Click the four numbers in the code to pick another color.

That's five lines of <Lang />, and none of them mentions a pipeline. Behind them, FragmentColor validates the WGSL and compiles it for your GPU. It builds a render pipeline, creates a buffer for `color` and copies your four numbers into it. Then it records the draw and submits it.

Let's read the shader from the top:

- `var<uniform> color: vec4<f32>` declares a **uniform**, a value the program sets from outside the shader. `vec4<f32>` means four 32-bit floats: red, green, blue and alpha. FragmentColor reads this declaration from the source and finds the uniform by its name, which is how `set("color", ...)` knows where the numbers go. The `@group(0) @binding(0)` in front is WGSL bookkeeping; the numbers only have to differ from one uniform to the next.
- `vs_main` is the vertex stage. A shader with no geometry attached draws three vertices, so `vs_main` runs three times, and `vertex_index` tells each run which corner it computes. The corners are in **normalized device coordinates**: `x` runs from -1 on the left edge to 1 on the right, `y` from -1 at the bottom to 1 at the top, whatever the target's size in pixels.
- `vs_main` returns its position in a `VOut` struct, marked `@builtin(position)`. The position has four numbers. After `x` and `y` come a depth, which decides what's in front when shapes overlap, and a value the GPU divides by to draw things in perspective. A flat drawing keeps them at `0.0` and `1.0`.
- `fs_main` is the fragment stage. It returns `color` for every pixel the triangle covers, and `@location(0)` sends that color to the target.

The triangle covers about a fifth of the canvas. On a 1920 × 1080 screen that's some 435,000 pixels, and the GPU works through thousands of them at once.

### Make it move

One call to `render` draws one frame. To animate, we set the uniform and render again every time the screen refreshes. Each step of this tutorial splits its code into two functions. `setup` runs once and builds the shader. `frame` runs on every refresh, about 60 times a second, and receives `time`, the seconds since the program started.

## Step 2: A second uniform

Look at the triangle in the demo above. Its corners describe a triangle with three equal sides, yet the canvas shows a wide, flat one.

Normalized device coordinates stretch -1 to 1 over each axis separately. This canvas is much wider than it is tall, so one unit along `x` spans far more pixels than one unit along `y`. On a phone held upright the canvas can turn taller than it is wide, and then the triangle comes out tall and thin.

The shader can't correct for a shape it doesn't know. A second uniform tells it:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/02-resolution-uniform/example.rs" region="shader" />

`resolution` holds the target's width and height in pixels. The vertex stage divides the width by the height to get the aspect ratio. Then it scales positions down along the longer axis to match the shorter one, and one unit covers the same number of pixels in both directions.

On the program side, `frame` reads the target's size and sets it next to the color:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/02-resolution-uniform/example.rs" region="frame" />

<Demo file="01-hello-triangle/02-resolution-uniform/example.rs" />

Three equal sides, on any canvas. From here on, every shader that places triangles declares `resolution`, and every `frame` sets it before it renders.

## Step 3: The shader library

The `Shader` constructor also takes a list. Each entry can be WGSL source, a path to a `.wgsl` file, a URL, or the name of a function in FragmentColor's [shader library](/shaders/). FragmentColor joins the entries in order and compiles them as one program.

The library holds more than 200 small WGSL functions: easing curves, color spaces, hashes, noise, distance functions in 2D and 3D, lighting models and post-processing effects. Each one has a name made of a category and a function, such as `noise/simplex2`. The other languages ship the whole library inside the package and look names up offline. On the web, a constructor can't wait for a download, so JavaScript loads a list with `await Shader.fetch(...)` instead.

Here `noise/simplex2` goes first in the list, ahead of our triangle's source:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/03-compose-noise/example.rs" region="setup" />

Now our fragment stage can call `simplex2` as if we had written it ourselves. Three other things are new in this shader:

- A third uniform, `time`, which `frame` sets on every refresh, right next to `color`.
- A second field in `VOut`, `uv`, marked `@location(0)`. Each corner computes a pair of numbers from 0 to 1, a common way to label points on a surface, and the GPU blends the three pairs across the triangle. Every pixel receives its own blend in `fs_main`.
- `.rgb` and `.a`, which pick channels out of a vector: `color.rgb` is the first three numbers of `color`, and `color.a` is the fourth.

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/03-compose-noise/example.rs" region="shader" />

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/03-compose-noise/example.rs" region="frame" />

Simplex noise gives a smooth, random-looking value for every point in the plane. The fragment stage samples it at each pixel's `uv`, moves the sample point a little further as `time` grows, and uses the result to dim and brighten the color. Light seems to ripple across the triangle, the way sunlight wavers on the floor of a swimming pool.

## Step 4: Bring your own vertices

So far the corners have lived inside the shader. Most programs keep their geometry on the CPU and send it to the GPU, and FragmentColor gives them two objects for that:

- **`Vertex`** is one point. Its constructor takes the position, and `set` attaches any other attribute by name, such as a color, a normal or texture coordinates.
- **`Mesh`** is a list of vertices.

We build three vertices, each with its own color, and attach the mesh to the shader:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/04-vertex-gradient/example.rs" region="setup" />

The positions have a third number, `z`, the depth. A flat triangle keeps it at `0.0`.

The vertex stage now declares two inputs, `position` at `@location(0)` and `color` at `@location(1)`. When we call <code><Lang rust="add_mesh" js="addMesh" py="add_mesh" swift="addMesh" kotlin="addMesh" /></code>, FragmentColor matches the vertex's position and its `color` attribute to those inputs by name, and checks every vertex. If a vertex lacks an attribute the shader reads, the call returns an error that names it. A plain number takes the type the shader declares, which makes each `color` a `vec3<f32>`.

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/04-vertex-gradient/example.rs" region="shader" />

`position.xy` picks the first two numbers of `position`, the way `color.rgb` did in step 3.

<Demo file="01-hello-triangle/04-vertex-gradient/example.rs" />

Pink, green and blue sit at the three corners. The GPU blends the vertex colors the way it blended `uv` in step 3: a pixel near the pink corner comes out mostly pink. One in the middle gets an even mix. Our program never mixes a color.

## Step 5: 15,000 triangles

One triangle is a warm-up. **Let's draw 15,000.**

One mesh is enough. The GPU can draw it 15,000 times, with each copy in its own place, with its own phase and tint. Those copies are **instances**, and an **`Instance`** holds the attributes of one copy the way a `Vertex` holds the attributes of one corner. Graphics programmers call small things drawn in large numbers **particles**, and the code uses that word from here on.

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/05-instances/example.rs" region="setup" />

The vertex stage reads both kinds of input. `position` and `color` change from corner to corner. We set `center`, `phase` and `tint` on an `Instance`, and that tells FragmentColor to change them from copy to copy while the three corners of one copy share them.

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/05-instances/example.rs" region="shader" />

Each copy sways around its `center` along a loop of its own. `phase` is the copy's head start along the sine waves, and the triangles reach the tops of their waves at different moments. `easing/in_out_sine` comes from the library this time. It reshapes the brightness wave, and each triangle rests at its brightest and its dimmest before it moves on.

Each frame sends only two values, `time` and `resolution`. From those two, the vertex stage places all 15,000 triangles:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/05-instances/example.rs" region="frame" />

<Demo file="01-hello-triangle/05-instances/example.rs" />

A **draw call** is one request from the CPU to the GPU to draw something. This one covers 45,000 vertices, every frame. Drawing each triangle with its own call would cost 15,000 requests instead.

## Step 6: A post-processing pass

For the last step, let's make it look like film. We render the particles into a texture first, then draw that texture to the screen through a second shader. The second shader grades the picture with four functions from the library:

- `postfx/chromatic_offsets` returns three slightly different spots to read red, green and blue from, the way a cheap lens splits colors near its edges.
- `color/tonemap_filmic` rolls off bright highlights the way film does, using a curve from Jim Hejl and Richard Burgess-Dawson.
- `postfx/vignette` darkens the corners.
- `postfx/film_grain` adds a new layer of noise on every frame.

Two more objects carry the picture from the first shader to the second:

- **`Texture`** is an image stored on the GPU. A `TextureTarget` is a target that keeps whatever is drawn into it as a `Texture` that another shader can read.
- **`Pass`** groups shaders and draws them into one target. `render` takes a `Shader` alone, as in the steps before, or a `Pass`, which adds two things: a target of its own and an order among passes. The name we give a pass shows up in error messages.

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/06-postfx-pass/example.rs" region="setup" />

<code><Lang rust="set_target" js="setTarget" py="set_target" swift="setTarget" kotlin="setTarget" /></code> points the particle pass at the texture, and <code><Lang rust="set_clear_color" js="setClearColor" py="set_clear_color" swift="setClearColor" kotlin="setClearColor" /></code> fills the texture with a dark blue before the particles draw. The output pass has no target of its own, so it draws to the target that `render` receives: the canvas. `require` says the output pass needs the particle pass to run first.

Now `frame` renders only the output pass. The renderer runs the particle pass first, because the output pass requires it:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/06-postfx-pass/example.rs" region="frame" />

Notice that `frame` gives the particle shader the canvas's size, not the texture's. The texture is a 1024 × 1024 square, and the post-processing shader stretches that square over the canvas. Squeezing each triangle by the canvas's aspect ratio beforehand cancels the stretch, and the particles keep their shape.

The post-processing shader covers the screen with a single triangle twice as wide and twice as tall as the screen. The GPU clips away the parts outside and runs `fs_main` once for every pixel inside:

<Snippet file="01-hello-triangle/06-postfx-pass/example.rs" region="postfx-shader" />

`scene` is the particle texture, and `textureSample` reads its color at a `uv` position. `scene_sampler` tells it how to blend neighboring pixels, and FragmentColor builds it from the texture's own settings: the program never sets it.

<Demo file="01-hello-triangle/06-postfx-pass/example.rs" />

The particle shader is the one from step 5, unchanged. Only its target moved.

## What just happened

In six steps we met the eight objects that do the drawing in FragmentColor:

- **`Renderer`** draws. Every step ends in the same `render` call.
- **`Target`** receives the picture: a window or canvas in every step, plus a texture in step 6.
- **`Shader`** is the program the GPU runs. Step 1 gives it a uniform, and steps 3, 5 and 6 add functions from the shader library.
- **`Vertex`** and **`Mesh`** move the geometry out of the shader and into our program in step 4.
- **`Instance`** turns one triangle into 15,000 in step 5.
- **`Pass`** and **`Texture`** chain two shaders in step 6: the first draws into a texture, and the second reads from it.
