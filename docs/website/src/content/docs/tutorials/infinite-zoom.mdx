Have you ever wondered what imaginary numbers look like?

Me neither, but it turns out they form beautiful shapes when you multiply them in sequence and organize them into a set based on how they behave. We will build a Mandelbrot Fractal together, make it zoomable until we hit a limit on your computer, then overcome that limit with clever maths to make it **infinitely** zoomable.

## An eternal shape nobody could see

Around 1918, Pierre Fatou and Gaston Julia studied what repeating a function like this one does to each starting point. They had proofs and pencils. A picture was out of reach: a single image of the set takes millions of these steps, and a person with paper might manage a few dozen points in an afternoon.

Robert Brooks and Peter Matelski printed [a rough picture of the set](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400881550-007) in 1978, one character at a time.

Two years later, Benoit Mandelbrot made it famous in his paper [FRACTAL ASPECTS OF THE ITERATION OF z →Λz(1- z) FOR COMPLEX Λ AND z](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.1980.tb29690.x), and then Adrien Douady and John Hubbard, who proved that the set is connected, named it after him.

Mandelbrot proved that there are geometrical objects, which he called Fractals, that are infinitely rough on all edges at any scale. Yet, these shapes are older than anyone in this story: They are eternal structures that humans discovered after some exploration. This story is important to computer science because we could only see these shapes with a computer. Visualizing them requires plotting millions of points in a plane, and computers are the tool that makes it possible.

## Drawing the fractal

The Mandelbrot set comes from one rule. It takes a complex number <Math tex="c" />, starts from <Math tex="z_0 = 0" />, and repeats

<Math block tex="z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c" />

over and over. The numbers it produces form the **orbit** of 0 for that <Math tex="c" />.

A complex number is a point in the plane: <Math tex="x + yi" /> is the point <Math tex="(x, y)" />. Squaring it gives <Math tex="(x^2 - y^2) + 2xy\,i" />, which turns one step of the rule into two lines of ordinary arithmetic. For some values of <Math tex="c" /> the orbit stays small forever. Others grow without limit, and any orbit that gets farther than 2 from the origin never returns. Every <Math tex="c" /> whose orbit stays small belongs to the **Mandelbrot set**.

Coloring each point of the plane by how many steps its orbit takes to escape draws a coastline of bulbs, spirals and filaments, with small, slightly distorted copies of the whole set at every magnification.

This tutorial builds a viewer for it in four steps. A single picture on the CPU comes first. Next, the same computation moves to the GPU, where rounding breaks the picture near `1e-6`. K. I. Martin's perturbation method then gets to `1e-35`, and a final step reaches `1e-1000` in real time. Those numbers measure the height of the view: at `1e-1000`, the canvas shows a strip of the plane <Math tex="10^{-1000}" /> units tall. Each step is a complete program in [`docs/tutorials/infinite-zoom`](https://github.com/vista-art/fragmentcolor/tree/main/docs/tutorials/infinite-zoom).

Rust writes the result to `mandelbrot.png`; Python, Swift and Kotlin write `mandelbrot.ppm`, and JavaScript draws into the first `<canvas>` on its page. The CPU handles one still image well enough. Smooth motion needs thirty to sixty of them every second, and deeper views need far more steps per pixel.

## Step 2: the same loop on the GPU

A GPU runs one small program on thousands of pixels in parallel, which suits this problem well. That program is a **fragment shader**: a function the GPU calls once for each pixel a triangle covers, written in WGSL, the shading language of WebGPU. Its numbers are `f32`, 32-bit floats. Complex numbers become `vec2<f32>`, with the real part in `x` and the imaginary part in `y`:

<Snippet file="infinite-zoom/02-on-the-gpu/example.rs" region="shader" />

`struct View` holds the shader's **uniforms**, inputs that stay the same for every pixel. The **host**, the program around the shader, sets them from the CPU. Four details are new:

- The escape radius is 256 instead of 2, and `65536` is its square. Past that radius, each step roughly squares the orbit's size, which doubles <Math tex="\log_2 |z|^2" />. `log2(log2(size2) / 16)` runs from 0, for an orbit that only just crossed the radius, to 1, for one that only just missed it on the step before (16 is <Math tex="\log_2 65536" />). Subtracting it from `n + 1` gives a count that changes smoothly from pixel to pixel, which blends the stripes of step 1 into gradients.
- `palette_iq` is the cosine palette from step 1, with its four constants as arguments.
- `print_depth` writes the view's height, as `1e-<depth>`, in the bottom left corner.
- `vs_main` places one triangle large enough to cover the whole canvas, since the fragment shader only runs where a triangle is. The GPU clips the parts outside.

Constants at the top set the target and the pace. `TARGET` is a spiral in Seahorse Valley, the crease between the big heart-shaped cardioid and the round bulb on its left:

### Seven digits

An `f32` stores a number as 24 significant bits, about seven decimal digits, times a power of two, <Math tex="2^e" />, where the integer <Math tex="e" /> is its **exponent**. Ordinary values have exponents from <Math tex="-126" /> to <Math tex="127" />, which covers everything from about <Math tex="10^{-38}" /> to <Math tex="10^{38}" />. Seven digits are all it ever has, though. Near <Math tex="-0.74" />, neighboring `f32` values are about `6e-8` apart along the real axis, and near <Math tex="0.13" /> about `1.5e-8` apart along the imaginary one. In a view `1e-6` tall, a pixel is far smaller than either gap. Blocks of pixels, dozens wide and a quarter as tall, round to the same <Math tex="c" /> and get the same color.

Multiplying keeps all seven digits: <Math tex="3 \times 10^{-30} \cdot 0.74" /> is as precise as its inputs. Adding a tiny number to a large one is where digits vanish, and in <Math tex="-0.74 + 3 \times 10^{-30}" /> the small part disappears completely.

Years ago I wrote a Mandelbrot explorer on CodePen that ran on the CPU, with JavaScript's 64-bit numbers. It drew until about `1e-13`, then showed the same blocks. Its WebGL version on Shadertoy ran much faster and failed sooner, around `1e-5`, because WebGL shaders only have 32-bit floats. WGSL has no 64-bit floats either. More bits would only delay the problem anyway: any fixed number of digits fails at some depth, because the set keeps its detail at every scale.

## Step 3: one careful orbit for every pixel

In 2013, K. I. Martin released SuperFractalThing, a Java program for deep zooms, and explained its speed in a two-page paper, SuperFractalThing Maths. Its original site is gone, and the [Internet Archive keeps a copy of the paper](https://web.archive.org/web/20130701075021/http://www.superfractalthing.co.nf/sft_maths.pdf). Martin's idea is **perturbation**: compute one orbit with all the digits it needs, then describe each pixel's orbit by its small difference from that one.

Here the notation changes. Capital <Math tex="Z_n" /> is the orbit of one carefully chosen point, the **reference**, and from now on <Math tex="c" /> means that point. A pixel lies a small offset <Math tex="\delta" /> away, at <Math tex="c + \delta" />, with orbit <Math tex="Z_n + z_n" />. Lowercase <Math tex="z_n" /> is now the pixel's difference from the reference. Both orbits follow the rule:

<Math block tex="Z_{n+1} + z_{n+1} = (Z_n + z_n)^2 + c + \delta = Z_n^2 + 2 Z_n z_n + z_n^2 + c + \delta" />

<Math block tex="Z_{n+1} = Z_n^2 + c" />

Subtracting the second line from the first leaves the rule for the difference:

<Math block tex="z_{n+1} = 2 Z_n z_n + z_n^2 + \delta" />

Arrows from each point of the reference to the pixel's point at the same step show the difference z_n, which grows longer at every step." caption="The reference orbit and one pixel's orbit, both from `0`. Every sum in this rule adds small numbers to small numbers. <Math tex="Z_n" /> enters it as a factor of <Math tex="z_n" />, and a product keeps all seven digits. The shader adds <Math tex="Z_n + z_n" /> only to test whether the pixel escaped, where a little rounding doesn't change the answer. Each pixel needs two inputs: its offset and the reference orbit. All the precision goes into that one orbit, computed once.

### The reference

The zoom's reference is a **Misiurewicz point**: after 24 steps, its orbit enters a cycle of period 2 and repeats it forever. Around such a point the set looks nearly the same at every depth, a spiral inside a spiral, so the zoom always has structure to show. Sixteen digits locate this one. Newton's method, in `refine`, improves them to as many as the deepest view needs, each round doubling the number of correct ones; on a first read, `refine` can stay a black box.

Those digits need more room than any float has. On the CPU, the reference stores them in integers of any size: `BigInt` in JavaScript, the `num-bigint` crate in Rust, `int` in Python, `BigInteger` in Kotlin and the [BigInt](https://github.com/attaswift/BigInt) package in Swift. A `BigInt` value `n` stands for <Math tex="n / 2^{\mathit{bits}}" />. A product of two such values has the factor <Math tex="2^{\mathit{bits}}" /> twice, and a right shift by `bits` removes one of them. `bits` is the deepest view's depth in binary digits plus 80 spare ones, which keeps rounding far below one pixel.

The host converts each <Math tex="Z_n" /> to `f32` as it goes. Inside the integers, every right shift also drops bits. That error grows along the orbit like the derivative <Math tex="dZ_n/dc" />, kept in `dr` and `di`, and the loop ends before it reaches <Math tex="2^{-40}" />.

In the shader, `dc` is the offset <Math tex="\delta" />. A second counter, `m`, is the pixel's position along the reference orbit; it matches the iteration count `n` until a rebase, explained below, separates the two. Each step updates `z` with the rule above. The reference joins it only for the tests, which compare the full value `orbit[m] + z` with the escape radius and with `z` itself:

### Glitches and rebasing

Martin's method has one weak spot. When a pixel's orbit passes close to 0 and the reference doesn't, <Math tex="Z_n" /> and <Math tex="z_n" /> nearly cancel. With <Math tex="Z_n = 0.5" /> and <Math tex="z_n = -0.4999999" />, the pixel's true value is <Math tex="10^{-7}" />, and only the last of the seven digits survives the subtraction. Every later step depends on that one digit. Whole regions come out as flat blobs called **glitches**, most often near the small copies of the set, where many orbits pass close to 0. Pauldelbrot, on Fractal Forums, found a test for them: a pixel has lost its digits once

<Math block tex="|Z_n + z_n| < 10^{-3}\, |Z_n|" />

Programs used it to find glitched pixels and render them again from a second reference.

In 2021, Zhuoran proposed a simpler fix, **rebasing**. As soon as the pixel is closer to 0 than to the reference, the shader sets `z` to the pixel's full value and `m` to 0. The difference rule only needs a point of the reference orbit that the pixel is close to, and at <Math tex="Z_0 = 0" /> the difference equals the full value. From there the pixel follows the start of the same orbit with its digits intact. That is the last `if` in the shader, which also catches a pixel that reaches the end of the reference orbit before it escapes. Rebasing costs one comparison per step and makes Pauldelbrot's test unnecessary, which is why the test appears here only as history.

### The smallest exponent

The smallest ordinary `f32` is <Math tex="2^{-126}" />, about `1.2e-38`. Below it come **denormal** values, which trade digits for range down to about `1.4e-45`, and many GPUs flush them to 0 for speed. A view `1e-35` tall on a canvas about 800 pixels high has pixels near `1e-38` wide. Once one pixel spans less than `1.2e-38`, `view.pixel` reads as 0 on such a GPU, every <Math tex="\delta" /> is 0, and every pixel follows the reference.

Here the exponent sets the limit; the digits still have room. An `f64` would extend the range to about `1e-308`, yet the spiral continues far beyond that.

## Step 4: past every exponent

Two problems stand between step 3 and `1e-1000`. The differences have to stay inside the range of an `f32`. Deeper views also need more iterations: near `1e-1000`, a pixel by this spiral takes about 32,000 steps to escape.

### Keep the exponent separately

Pauldelbrot described the first fix as **rescaled iterations**. Each difference becomes a pair: a `vec2<f32>` `w` near 1, and an integer exponent `s`, with <Math tex="z = w \cdot 2^s" />. Dividing the rule from step 3 by <Math tex="2^s" /> gives

<Math block tex="w_{n+1} = 2 Z_n w_n + 2^s\, w_n^2 + 2^{-s}\,\delta" />

Only the factor <Math tex="2^s" /> and the scaled offset <Math tex="2^{-s}\delta" /> can leave the range of an `f32`. When they do, they are negligible compared with <Math tex="2 Z_n w_n" />, and rounding them to 0 loses nothing, except right after a rebase. The shader keeps them in `unit` and `offset` and recomputes both only when `s` changes. Three more details complete the loop:

- Whenever `w` grows past `65536` or shrinks below `1/65536`, the shader moves a power of two from `w` into `s`. WGSL's `frexp` splits a number into its digits and its power of two, and `ldexp` multiplies by a power of two; the `scale` helper wraps `ldexp`.
- Right after a rebase, <Math tex="Z_0 = 0" /> and the first term is 0 as well. The `m == 0u` branch computes that step from the square and the offset alone and picks a new `s` for the result.
- The pixel's offset arrives in the same form, as a `vec2<f32>` `d` times <Math tex="2^e" />, where `e` is `view.exponent`.

### Skip what every pixel shares

The second fix comes from Martin's paper. Writing out the first steps of the rule from step 3, starting from <Math tex="z_0 = 0" />, shows a pattern:

<Math block tex="z_1 = \delta, \qquad z_2 = 2 Z_1 \delta + \delta^2 + \delta = (2 Z_1 + 1)\,\delta + \delta^2" />

Every step of the rule multiplies and adds powers of <Math tex="\delta" />, which makes <Math tex="z_n" /> a polynomial in <Math tex="\delta" />. For thousands of iterations, <Math tex="\delta" /> is so small that the first three terms of this **series** hold nearly all of it:

<Math block tex="z_n \approx A_n \delta + B_n \delta^2 + C_n \delta^3" />

Substituting this into the rule and collecting each power of <Math tex="\delta" /> gives coefficients that don't depend on the pixel:

<Math block tex="A_{n+1} = 2 Z_n A_n + 1, \quad B_{n+1} = 2 Z_n B_n + A_n^2, \quad C_{n+1} = 2 Z_n C_n + 2 A_n B_n" />

They start from <Math tex="A_0 = B_0 = C_0 = 0" />. <Math tex="A_n" /> is the derivative <Math tex="dZ_n/dc" /> from step 3, the same quantity that tracks the reference's rounding error. (Martin's paper starts its orbit at <Math tex="X_0 = c" />; its indices run one lower than these.) The CPU computes the coefficients once, alongside the orbit. They grow past the largest `f64`, so the CPU stores each as a number and a separate power of two, like the shader's <Math tex="w \cdot 2^s" />: a small type called `Wide` in Rust, Swift and Kotlin, and a `[re, im, exponent]` triple in JavaScript and Python.

The series holds while the first term it drops, <Math tex="D_n \delta^4" />, stays small compared with <Math tex="A_n \delta" />. Its coefficient follows the same pattern, <Math tex="D_{n+1} = 2 Z_n D_n + 2 A_n C_n + B_n^2" />. For every iteration, the CPU records the widest radius at which that term stays below a ten-millionth of the first, close to the precision of an `f32`:

### A compute pass and a render pass

The iteration moves into a **compute shader**: GPU code that runs as a grid of invocations and writes its results to memory, with no triangles involved. Splitting the work this way lets the expensive part run at a lower resolution on a large screen, while a cheap second shader turns its results into colors. `@workgroup_size(8, 8)` groups the invocations into tiles of 8 by 8, `global_invocation_id` tells each one which pixel it handles, and the host's `set_compute_dispatch` (`setComputeDispatch` in JavaScript) asks for enough tiles to cover the image. Each invocation writes one smooth count into a storage buffer, `counts`.

FragmentColor runs shaders in **passes**. A `Pass` holds shaders that draw or compute together, and `render` takes a list of passes and runs them in order; steps 2 and 3 handed `render` a lone `Shader`, which acts as a pass of its own. Any pass whose shader has a `@compute` entry point runs as a compute pass. The second pass reads `counts` and colors each pixel, with the palette's density and phase and the inside color as its uniforms. Comments such as `// 100..5000` give the [playground](/welcome/playground/#mandelbrot)'s sliders their ranges.

## Where to go next

The [playground](/welcome/playground/#mandelbrot) runs the last program with both shaders and its JavaScript host open for editing. Some changes to try there:

- Set `TARGET` to another Misiurewicz point. Other preperiods and periods work, as long as the sixteen digits start close enough for Newton's method; from too far away, it can settle on a nearby point with a shorter preperiod or period.
- Raise `DEPTH`. Each extra power of ten adds about 32 orbit iterations to `setup`, each on wider integers. Past roughly `1e-2000`, the orbit needs a larger `ORBIT`. The GPU work per frame stays the same.
- Replace the series with **bilinear approximation**, from the same 2021 posts in which Zhuoran introduced rebasing. It also skips iterations after a rebase, and current deep zoom programs extend it. Claude Heiland-Allen's [Deep zoom theory and practice (again)](https://mathr.co.uk/blog/2022-02-21_deep_zoom_theory_and_practice_again.html) explains it.
