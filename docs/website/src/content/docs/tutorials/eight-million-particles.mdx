[Hello, million triangles!](/tutorials/hello-triangle/) comes first. It ends with the GPU placing a million triangles, each from nothing more than its `instance_index`. This tutorial assumes you know the `Shader`, `Pass` and `require` calls and the full-screen triangle from that page, and it reuses the `hash`, `random` and `fit` lines of its step 7 unchanged.

Somewhere past a million, triangles stop paying for themselves. Shrinking one saves little, because at four pixels wide most of its cost goes into preparing it, before any pixel gets a color.

**Here, eight million particles move every frame, and none of them is a triangle.**

## Compute shaders and storage buffers

In this program, a **compute shader** moves every particle and counts it in a grid of 1280 × 720 cells. One full-screen triangle then colors each pixel by the count in its cell: the more particles a cell holds, the brighter it glows.

A compute shader runs a function for every item, in workgroups of 256 here. It reads and writes **storage buffers**: arrays on the GPU that keep their contents from frame to frame. One of them stores our particles, each as a position and a velocity. A second holds a count per grid cell. FragmentColor keeps one storage buffer per name and binds it to every shader that declares it. The `clear`, `simulate` and `draw` shaders all reach the same cells through `counts`.

## Three passes per frame

<Snippet file="eight-million-particles/01-splat/example.rs" region="setup" />

The `sizes` string holds three WGSL constants, formatted from <Lang /> numbers. Each `Shader` list puts them ahead of the shader's own source, as the first tutorial's step 3 does with `noise/simplex2`. `COUNT` makes the particle buffer eight million entries of 16 bytes, 128 MB in all. That's just under 128 MiB (134,217,728 bytes), and every WebGPU device must allow a storage buffer binding up to that size.

A `Pass` made from a compute shader dispatches workgroups instead of drawing. <code><Lang rust="set_compute_dispatch" js="setComputeDispatch" py="set_compute_dispatch" swift="setComputeDispatch" kotlin="setComputeDispatch" /></code> says how many, in up to three dimensions: enough groups of 256 along the first to cover every item, and 1 along the other two. The `require` calls chain the passes in order, and `frame` hands only the last one to `render`.

## Moving the particles

<Snippet file="eight-million-particles/01-splat/example.rs" region="simulate-shader" />

`global_invocation_id` numbers the runs, as `instance_index` numbers the copies of a mesh.

The ID is a `vec3<u32>` to match the three dispatch dimensions, and a flat list of particles needs only `id.x`. Rounding the dispatch up can start a few runs past the end. The `if` at the top of each compute shader skips them.

Every new storage buffer holds zeros. A particle standing perfectly still takes that as its cue to pick an orbit. After that, every frame adds the pull of the **attractor**, a moving point near the middle, to the velocity. The particle then moves by that velocity for 1/60 of a second. About one particle in 300 also starts over on each frame, which keeps the attractor from swallowing them all.

The grid is a flat array, one row after another: the cell in column `x` and row `y` is element `y * GRID.x + x`.

More than a thousand particles can share a cell during one frame. `atomicAdd` makes sure each of them counts, where an ordinary `+=` from several runs at once could lose some. Like `atomicStore` in the first pass, it takes a pointer to the cell, written with `&`.

## Drawing the counts

<Snippet file="eight-million-particles/01-splat/example.rs" region="draw-shader" />

The `draw` shader just reads, so it declares `counts` as plain `u32` with `read` access: the same bytes without the `atomic` wrapper. In a fragment stage, `@builtin(position)` holds the pixel's coordinates in the target, counted from the top-left corner, which is why `fs_main` flips `y` before it looks up a cell. The grid stretches to fill the canvas, while `fit` in the `simulate` shader keeps the swirl round. Only at 1280 × 720 does a cell match exactly one pixel.

## Where the time goes

A particle here is one run of the compute shader and one `atomicAdd`, with no triangle for the GPU to prepare. Item for item, it costs about a sixteenth as much as an instanced triangle. This program moves eight times as many particles as step 7 of the first tutorial draws triangles, in about half the time.

Your own numbers depend on your GPU. The [frame budget](/tutorials/hello-triangle/#your-frame-budget) section of the first tutorial shows how to measure them, and `FC_TUTORIAL_FRAMES=120 ./fc example tutorial_eight_million_particles_splat` prints this program's average frame time.

## What just happened

- A **compute shader** runs a function per item and needs no geometry.
- **Storage buffers** keep their data on the GPU between frames, and every shader that declares one by name shares it.
- `atomicAdd` lets many runs count into the same cell without losing any.
- One full-screen draw turns the counts into a picture.
